TRANSPARENCY / ASSUMPTIONS

Methodology & Limitations

Every metric in this lab is reproducible from supplied assumptions. None of the results represent verified customer savings.

Model boundary

Facility electricity = IT electricity × PUE
Operational tCO₂e = Facility kWh × assumed grid kgCO₂e/kWh ÷ 1,000
Baseline total tCO₂e = Operational tCO₂e + optional allocated embodied tCO₂e
Scenario IT kWh = Baseline IT kWh × (1 − efficiency / 100) × (1 + rebound / 100)
Scenario operational tCO₂e = Scenario IT kWh × PUE × factor ÷ 1,000
Scenario total tCO₂e = Scenario operational tCO₂e + optional unchanged allocated embodied tCO₂e

Assumes unchanged regional factor, PUE and embodied allocation in scenario. This is not a time-resolved or consequential LCA model.

Interpreting the numbers

Energy reductions do not guarantee emissions reductions if demand increases. Rebound is modeled as a multiplicative change in workload demand.

Location-based factors are illustrative; marginal carbon intensity for scheduling decisions is a different quantity. Never conflate both.

Embodied carbon is an illustrative annual workload allocation, not an authoritative Scope 3 inventory or purchase avoidance credit.

PUE applies only to IT power. If input energy already includes facility overhead, setting PUE above 1 would double count it.

Financial scenarios assume a simple electricity tariff; cloud billing, demand charges, migration costs and depreciation are excluded unless explicitly modeled.

Reference alignment & product status

Suggested methodology references include the Green Software Foundation / ISO/IEC 21031 Software Carbon Intensity specification, the GHG Protocol Scope 2 Guidance, the GHG Protocol Corporate Value Chain (Scope 3) Standard, and the ISO 14064 family. These inform future design, but UMAN-ECO is not certified or validated against them.

Production roadmap: real cloud and on-prem connectors → validated factor provenance and time-series modeling → tenant isolation and SSO → governed change execution → independent verification of outcomes.

Current release: standalone educational prototype. Browser storage only; no server-side processing, analytics, secrets, or external API calls.

SUS-701 ASSIGNMENT 4 / GREEN ENTREPRENEURSHIP

Venture & Sustainability Impact

UMAN-ECO is an academic concept for a mobile-friendly, business-focused carbon-footprint tracking platform. The business case below illustrates a possible venture; it is not an offer of services or a commercial launch.

01 / PROBLEM DEFINITION

Why the problem matters

Organizations with cloud, AI and data-center workloads may lack clear electricity and emissions visibility at business-unit level. Without periodic records, efficiency opportunities and changing consumption are difficult to compare.

Local + global relevance: business electricity costs, local grid mix and broader digital-sector energy demand.

02 / INNOVATION & VALUE

From visibility to decisions

Combine monthly tracking, cost-and-carbon scenario exploration, and explainable GreenOps recommendations. Environmental value: identify potential energy reductions. Social value: accessible sustainability literacy and greater transparency. Economic value: potential waste reduction and advisory use cases.

03 / BUSINESS MODEL

Who pays, and why?

Target customer: SMEs, IT departments, managed-service providers and enterprise sustainability teams. Value proposition: a practical shared view of energy, emissions and operational trade-offs. Potential revenue: tiered SaaS subscriptions, implementation support and analytics advisory. Costs: engineering, data licensing, hosting, security, support and validation.

04 / ENVIRONMENTAL ASSESSMENT

Reduce operational waste

Resources include software engineering, energy data, grid factors, hosting and user training. Potential interventions include right-sizing, scheduling, equipment-life extension and avoiding unnecessary capacity procurement. Any circular-economy benefit must be substantiated rather than assumed.

05 / STAKEHOLDERS

A cooperative model

Business customers, platform/SRE teams, FinOps, sustainability professionals, academic partners, electricity providers and data partners. Governments and NGOs could provide education, standards guidance or relevant programs, depending on jurisdiction and eligibility; no partnership is claimed.

06 / POLICY & GOVERNANCE

Trustworthy boundaries

Real deployment would require privacy, information security and jurisdiction-specific environmental reporting assessments. Reference frameworks include GHG Protocol Scope 2 and ISO/IEC 21031 for software carbon intensity; referencing them is not certification. Taxes, subsidies and incentives require location-specific eligibility checks.

07 / IMPACT & MEASUREMENT

What success could mean

Environmental: estimated kWh, tCO₂e, intensity and data-quality coverage. Social: user adoption, sustainability-literacy sessions and accessibility. Economic: customer retention, cost-to-serve, modeled electricity savings and revenue. Actual claims require independent evidence.

08 / SCALE & RISKS

A staged path

Start with a sample-data mobile web prototype, then controlled pilots with validated metering and factor provenance, followed by secure production integrations if viability is demonstrated. Challenges include data access, double counting, rebound effects, regional comparability, cybersecurity and customer acquisition.
